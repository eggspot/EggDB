## D621 SQL Server's DENY — Decided by the code-only session (2026-11-12, D355); roadmap M6b, D285, D586

D586 gave Transact-SQL `GRANT` and `REVOKE` on the shared privilege model and left `DENY`
refused. SQL Server schemas use `DENY` to carve exceptions out of broad grants: "everyone may
read, except the `contractor` role on `salaries`". A database that only accepted the grant
would let contractors read.

- `DENY privileges ON [OBJECT::]table TO principals` is parsed as `GRANT` is, and becomes
  `RoleStatement::Deny`. Denying on a schema, on columns or of role membership is refused as not
  supported.
- Denies are kept per table and principal under a new key space (`DENY_SPACE`, 0x18), one byte of
  privilege bits beside the grants'.
  - Denying a privilege also removes that principal's grant of it.
  - `GRANT` clears the principal's deny for what it grants, and `REVOKE` clears both grant and
    deny, as SQL Server's `REVOKE` removes either.
  - A dropped table's denies go with it.
- A deny wins: before any grant is looked at, the table check asks whether the role, any role it
  belongs to, or `PUBLIC` is denied the privilege.
  - The column check does the same, so a table-level `DENY SELECT` blocks a column grant too.
  - Superuser sessions (the server's own user, embedded use) are not checked, as SQL Server's
    `sysadmin` is not subject to `DENY`.
- The catalog's access records include the denies, so `has_table_privilege` and
  `has_column_privilege` answer the way statements are checked.
- The Transact-SQL grant test now checks that a deny beats a grant to `public`, that `REVOKE`
  lifts it, and that a deny on a schema is refused.

Not done: `DENY` on schemas, columns and database-level permissions, and `WITH CASCADE`.
