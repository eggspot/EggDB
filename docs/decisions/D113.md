## D113 time, timetz, interval, bytea, uuid and character(n) in the one type system; table definitions in format version 5; random bytes from the embedding program — Decided by the build loop (by judgement, 2026-10-27); applies D1, D8, D9, D10, D19, D20, D111

Item 31 adds six PostgreSQL types to the type system every dialect shares.

**time and timetz.** A `time` is microseconds since midnight, 0 to 86,400,000,000 (`24:00:00`
is allowed, as in PostgreSQL), read in ISO form (`04:05:06.789`, `04:05 PM`, a date-time string's
time part) and printed `HH:MM:SS[.ffffff]`; `time(p)` rounds half up. A `timetz` keeps the time
and its offset east of UTC (at most 15:59:59 either way); one without an offset takes the
session's. Two `timetz` values compare by their UTC time and then, for the same instant, the
one further west sorts higher, as PostgreSQL's ordering does, so `10:00+01` and `09:00+00` are
not equal. `timetz` is minimal: no time zone conversion functions (`AT TIME ZONE`) yet.

**interval** is PostgreSQL's triple: 32-bit months, 32-bit days and 64-bit microseconds, kept
apart because their lengths depend on the date they are added to. Input takes PostgreSQL's
format (`1 year 2 mons 3 days 04:05:06`, units from microseconds to millennia with
abbreviations, fractions spilling down by 12 months a year, 30 days a month and 24 hours a
day, `ago`, the SQL standard's `1-2` and `3 4:05:06`) and ISO 8601's designators
(`P1Y2M3DT4H5M6S`, `P2W`); output is the `postgres` interval style. The fields restriction
(`interval year to month`, `day to second(3)` and the other eleven) truncates as PostgreSQL's
does and gives a bare number its unit. Values compare, hash, group and are keyed by their
length in microseconds with a month counted as 30 days and a day as 24 hours, exactly
PostgreSQL's interval equality: `'1 mon' = '30 days' = '720 hours'`, a unique index holds one of
them, and `DISTINCT` keeps whichever came first (PostgreSQL leaves that choice open too).
Arithmetic follows PostgreSQL's code paths as its manual describes them: `+`/`-` field by field
with overflow checked (`22008 interval out of range`), `*` and `/` by a number spill fractional
months into days and fractional days into microseconds rounded to the microsecond, and a NaN,
an infinity or an out-of-range result is `22008` (PostgreSQL 17's infinite intervals wait);
adding months to a time stamp moves the calendar month and clamps the day to the month's end
(`2024-01-31 + 1 mon = 2024-02-29`), then days, then microseconds, each step range-checked; a
`timestamptz` does its calendar arithmetic in the session's time zone. `timestamp - timestamp` is
an interval in days and microseconds (`justify_hours`), `age` is PostgreSQL's field-by-field
difference with borrowing from the earlier operand's month. `justify_days`, `justify_hours`,
`justify_interval` and `extract`/`date_part` of an interval work; `week` and the day-of fields
answer `0A000` as in PostgreSQL. Interval text input is capped at 4,096 bytes and every number in
it at 2^64, so a hostile literal can neither overflow nor run long.

**bytea** reads the hex format (`\x0aff`, whitespace between digit pairs, an odd digit count is
`22023`) and the escape format (`\\`, `\ooo` octal, anything else after a backslash `22P02`),
prints hex, compares byte by byte, and goes with `||`, `length`/`octet_length`, `substring`,
`get_byte`, `encode` and `decode` (`hex`, `base64` with PostgreSQL's 76-character lines,
`escape`). Every bytea result is checked against a 1,000,000,000-byte limit before it is
allocated (`54000`), so `||`, `encode` (up to four times its input) and `decode` cannot be
used to grow memory without limit.

**uuid** is 16 bytes, read in every form PostgreSQL accepts (upper or lower case, braces, no
hyphens, a hyphen after any group of four digits), printed lower-case and hyphenated, and
compared byte by byte. `gen_random_uuid()` makes a version 4 UUID (RFC 9562) from 16 random
bytes. The SQL core never reaches the operating system itself (D8): it draws random bytes from
a `RandomSource` the embedding program registers once (`eggdb_sql::set_random_source`); the
`eggdb` binary and `eggdb::Database`'s SQL store register `eggdb_platform::secure_random::fill`,
the source the SCRAM nonces use. A program that registers none gets `0A000`.

**character(n)** (`char(n)`, `bpchar`) stores its text padded with spaces to `n` characters
(`char` alone is `char(1)`, `bpchar` alone has no length and no padding). Assigning a longer
value fails with `22001` unless the excess is spaces; a cast truncates. Trailing spaces do not
count when it is compared, hashed, grouped or keyed, and are removed when it becomes `text`
(`||`, `lower`, `LIKE`), while `length` ignores them and `octet_length` counts them, as
PostgreSQL's manual says. A `character` compared with `text` compares as text, so
`'ab'::char(4) = 'ab'` is true and `= 'ab '::text` is false, and equality keys agree with that.

**Dialects.** The new typed literals (`time '...'` aside) and functions are PostgreSQL's alone:
`interval`, `bytea`, `uuid`, `timetz` and `bpchar` literals and `encode`, `decode`, `get_byte`,
`substring`, `justify_*`, `age`, `gen_random_uuid`, `current_time` and `localtime` answer
"does not exist" or a syntax error elsewhere. MySQL's `TIME(fsp)` is `time(fsp)` (its range
`-838:59:59` to `838:59:59` and day counts are refused outside PostgreSQL's 0 to 24 hours, a
known difference) and `TIME '...'` is its literal; `BLOB`, `TINYBLOB`, `MEDIUMBLOB`,
`LONGBLOB`, `BINARY(n)` and `VARBINARY(n)` are `bytea` (no zero padding of `BINARY` and no
length check yet); MySQL's `CHAR(n)` stays `varchar(n)` as before, which already matches MySQL's
"trailing spaces removed when read". Oracle's `CHAR(n)` and `NCHAR(n)` become blank-padded
`character(n)` (Oracle pads and compares them the same way; its `LENGTH` counts the padding, as
Oracle's does), and `RAW(n)`, `LONG RAW` and `BLOB` are `bytea`; Oracle's `INTERVAL` types wait.
A string becomes bytes by each dialect's rule, set for the statement the session runs:
PostgreSQL reads its `bytea` input syntax, MySQL (and SQLite) take the string's bytes as they
are, and Oracle reads hexadecimal digits as `HEXTORAW` does (`ORA-01465` otherwise); bytes become
text the opposite way (PostgreSQL's hex, MySQL's bytes as UTF-8, Oracle's upper-case hex). A
bound parameter declared `bytea` is always written in PostgreSQL's input form, whatever the
dialect, so a client can bind any bytes. MySQL's `CONCAT` with a binary argument returns bytes.
SQLite keeps type affinity: a declared `BLOB` column is still refused, because a SQLite column
holds any storage class and a typed `bytea` column would refuse the integers and text SQLite
accepts there; it waits for a dynamic-value column.

**Wire.** PostgreSQL's protocol sends the types with OIDs 1083, 1266, 1186, 17, 2950 and 1042,
in text and in binary (time as 8 bytes, timetz with its offset in seconds west, interval as
microseconds, days, months, bytea raw, uuid as 16 bytes), and reads binary parameters of each
with their lengths and ranges checked (`22P03`). MySQL's sends `TIME` columns as
`MYSQL_TYPE_TIME` (packed in the binary protocol) and `bytea` as `MYSQL_TYPE_BLOB` with the
binary collation, raw bytes in both protocols; a bound parameter sent with a `BLOB` type code,
or whose bytes are not UTF-8, is taken as a `bytea`, and a string parameter as text, whose bytes a
binary column stores unchanged. `CURTIME()` and `CURRENT_TIME` return the session's local time to
the second.

**File format.** A table definition with a column of a new type is written in version 5: the
version 4 layout with the type tags 15 (`time`) and 16 (`timetz`, each then one byte of
fractional digits, `0xff` without), 17 (`interval`, then its fields code 0 to 13 and its
precision byte; a precision with fields that do not end in seconds is damage), 18 (`bytea`), 19
(`uuid`) and 20 (`character`, then its length as 32 bits, 0 without, at most 10,485,760).
Tables without them keep their version and their exact bytes; versions 1 to 4 decode as before
and refuse the new tags, and an older release refuses a version 5 table by name. Rows keep
format 1 with the same tags as value tags: a time is 8 bytes (checked to its range), a timetz 8
and 4 (checked), an interval 4, 4 and 8, a uuid 16, `bytea` and `character` length-prefixed
(`character` checked as UTF-8). Index keys are order-preserving and the key bytes of earlier
types do not change: a time is its microseconds sign-flipped, a timetz its UTC microseconds and
its offset west, an interval its 128-bit length sign-flipped (equal intervals share one key; a
key decodes to a canonical interval of the same length, which is all the index check needs),
a uuid its 16 bytes, `bytea` and `character` (without trailing spaces) escaped like text. A
second, security-focused review of this format change follows the PR review (D35).

Judgement calls, made without asking (CLAUDE.md, "Questions"): the 4,096-byte interval input
cap; the bytea size limit (the one SQLite's functions use); `DISTINCT` and `min`/`max` returning
the first of several equal intervals; Oracle's `CHAR` changing to `character(n)` for new tables
(existing tables keep `varchar`); MySQL's `CHAR` staying `varchar`; SQLite's `BLOB` staying
refused; MySQL's out-of-day `TIME` values refused rather than stored as intervals; a global,
set-once random source rather than one per session.

Note (D149, item 42b): Oracle's `INTERVAL YEAR TO MONTH` and `INTERVAL DAY TO SECOND` are now `interval year to month` and `interval day to second(s)` in the Oracle dialect.

**Superseded in part by D153.** Infinite intervals, MySQL's `TIME` from `-838:59:59` to `838:59:59` and SQLite `BLOB` columns now exist.
